Host: Pricing changes seem to terrify founders more than almost any other decision. Why is that
fear justified or not?

Renata: It's justified, but usually for the wrong reason. Founders fear the spreadsheet math —
"will this number go up or down" — when the bigger risk is trust. A pricing change communicated
badly can make existing customers feel like the rules changed on them without warning, and that
damages retention far more than getting the actual price point slightly wrong. Get the
communication right and you can survive an imperfect price. Get the communication wrong and even
a perfect price won't save you.

Host: What does "communicated badly" look like in practice?

Renata: Silent changes that customers discover on their renewal invoice. Even if your terms of
service technically allowed it, discovering a price increase via a surprise charge reads as
dishonest, regardless of intent. The version that works: proactive email well before the renewal,
clear reasoning for the change, and — ideally — grandfathering existing customers on their current
price for some fixed window, even if new customers pay the new price immediately.

Host: How do you decide when it's time to introduce usage-based pricing versus staying with flat
seat-based pricing?

Renata: Usage-based pricing works when value and usage genuinely scale together and your
customers can predict their usage well enough to budget for it. It fails when customers can't
forecast their bill, because unpredictable bills create renewal anxiety even among customers who
are otherwise happy. If you're considering usage-based pricing, the real prerequisite isn't a
metering system — it's confidence that a typical customer's usage doesn't swing wildly month to
month. If it does, a hybrid model with a usage-based tier on top of a predictable base fee
usually serves customers better than pure usage pricing.

Host: What's your view on freemium versus a free trial for new products?

Renata: It depends entirely on how quickly value shows up. Freemium works when the free tier can
demonstrate real value on its own and the paid tier is a genuine expansion of that value —
more seats, more volume, advanced features layered on a useful free core. A free trial works
better when the core value only shows up with real usage over time, because a trial creates
urgency to actually use the product before access disappears. Slapping freemium onto a product
whose value only appears after weeks of usage just produces a large, unconverting free tier that
costs you support and infrastructure without ever showing users the payoff.

Host: How should teams think about discounting, especially for enterprise deals?

Renata: Every discount should have a stated reason attached to it internally, not just "we needed
to close the deal." Multi-year commitment, expanded seat count, a logo you want for the reference
list — those are defensible reasons. "The rep really wanted this quarter's number" is not, and it
trains your sales team and your customers that list price is fictional, which erodes pricing power
across the whole pipeline over time, not just on that one deal.

Host: What's a pricing mistake you see repeatedly, even at experienced companies?

Renata: Pricing based on cost or competitor prices instead of value delivered. I've seen teams
set a price by looking at three competitors and picking something in the middle, without ever
quantifying what the product actually saves or earns their customer. If you can tie your price to
a customer's own value calculation — "this saves your team roughly 10 hours a week" — you have a
defensible, expandable price. If your price is just "slightly less than competitor X," you have no
floor and no ceiling, and you'll be stuck reacting to competitor pricing forever instead of setting
your own.

Host: Any advice for founders about to raise prices for the first time?

Renata: Test the new price on new customers before touching existing ones. It's a much lower-
risk way to learn whether the market will bear the increase, and it buys you real data before you
have the harder conversation with your existing base. By the time you roll it out to existing
customers, you're not guessing anymore — you already know conversion held up at the new price
point.
