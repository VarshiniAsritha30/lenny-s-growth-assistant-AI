Host: Let's start with the question everyone asks. How do you actually know when you've hit
product-market fit, versus just having a few enthusiastic early users?

Dana: The signal I trust most isn't a survey score, it's behavior you didn't have to ask for.
When we hit product-market fit at my last company, support tickets started including phrases
like "how do I get my whole team on this" without us ever pitching a team plan. Users were
selling the product to their own coworkers before we'd built anything to support that. That's
very different from ten people telling you on a call that they "love" the product.

Host: A lot of founders point to the Sean Ellis test — the "how would you feel if you could no
longer use this product" survey, with 40% saying "very disappointed" as the bar. Do you use
that?

Dana: I use it as one input, not the verdict. The problem with the 40% test in isolation is that
it's easy to hit it with a narrow, self-selected group of power users while the broader market
shrugs. I'd rather pair it with retention curves. If your week-8 retention curve is still
sloping downward, you don't have product-market fit yet, no matter what the survey says. A flat
retention curve — even a low one, say 25% — with organic pull is a much stronger signal than a
high satisfaction score with a curve that's still decaying toward zero.

Host: What's the biggest mistake you see teams make while searching for fit?

Dana: Optimizing the wrong loop. Teams pour effort into acquisition — ads, SEO, outbound — before
they've proven the core loop retains anyone. You end up with a leaky bucket that just leaks
faster. My rule: don't spend a dollar on acquisition until you can point to a cohort, even a
small one, whose usage is flat or growing month over month. Otherwise you're just paying to
learn the same lesson at a larger, more expensive scale.

Host: How long should a team give itself to find fit before pivoting?

Dana: I'm suspicious of fixed timelines like "18 months" because they encourage grinding on a
dead thesis just because the clock hasn't run out. What I look at instead is whether each
iteration is moving a real number — activation rate, week-4 retention, whatever your core metric
is. If three consecutive iterations move that number by less than your measurement noise, that's
the pivot signal, whether it's month 4 or month 20. If the number is moving, even slowly, keep
going.

Host: Any advice for teams that are pre-launch and trying to increase their odds of finding fit
faster?

Dana: Talk to users before you write code, but don't just ask what they want — watch what they
currently do to solve the problem badly. If someone has cobbled together a working process out
of a spreadsheet, three Slack channels, and a calendar reminder, that's gold. It tells you the
problem is painful enough that people are already paying a "tax" in time and workarounds. Your
job is to be cheaper than that tax. If nobody has a duct-taped workaround, that's often a sign
the problem isn't painful enough yet to build a company around.

Host: Let's talk about niche versus broad. Should early-stage teams narrow their target user
aggressively?

Dana: Almost always yes. The teams that struggle to find fit are usually trying to build
something for "product managers" broadly, when the real opportunity is "product managers at
Series B SaaS companies who just inherited a legacy pricing model." The narrower ICP lets you
write copy, features, and onboarding that feels like it was built specifically for that person,
because it was. You can always widen later once the core loop is proven — widening a proven loop
is a much easier problem than trying to find a loop that works for everyone at once.

Host: Last question — what's one thing you'd tell a founder who thinks they have fit but isn't
sure?

Dana: Try to stop doing something that's working. If you paused a manual, unscalable thing you've
been doing to keep users happy — a personal onboarding call, a hand-written setup guide — and
usage doesn't drop, you might just have a feature people tolerate, not a product they need. Real
fit tends to survive you stepping back a little. If it doesn't survive that, you were the product,
not the software.
