Host: If a company is making its first growth hire, what should that person's background look
like?

Tomasz: Generalist first, specialist second — and comfortable being uncomfortable with ambiguity.
The first growth hire is going to touch analytics, run experiments, write copy, and occasionally
argue with engineering about instrumentation, often in the same week. A specialist paid-
acquisition hire is fantastic at company number thirty in the growth org, but as employee one
they'll be excellent at one lever and stuck everywhere else. I look for people who've shipped
something end-to-end themselves, even something small and scrappy, over someone with a big title
at a big company who managed a team that did the actual work.

Host: How do you evaluate for that "generalist who can execute" trait in an interview?

Tomasz: I ask them to walk through one experiment they ran, in painful detail — not the result,
the process. How did they pick the metric, what did the instrumentation actually look like, what
went wrong, how did they know when to call it. People who've genuinely done the work light up
telling this story and volunteer the messy parts unprompted. People who managed it at a distance
tend to describe only the clean, retrospective version, and start hedging when you push on
specifics like sample size or how they handled a confounding variable.

Host: When should a company build a dedicated growth team versus embedding growth
responsibilities into the core product team?

Tomasz: It depends on whether you have a proven core loop yet. Before product-market fit, a
separate growth team is often a mistake — it creates a team optimizing acquisition and
activation for a product that doesn't retain, which just produces expensive, well-measured churn.
After fit, a focused team makes sense because you now have a real loop worth optimizing
relentlessly, and that requires someone whose only job is to look for leaks in it, rather than
someone splitting attention across ten other product priorities.

Host: How big should a growth team be at, say, 20 people total in the company?

Tomasz: Usually one, maybe two people, and they should be embedded closely with a product
engineer and a designer rather than isolated in their own silo. At that stage, growth's job is
mostly to find the highest-leverage lever and get it prioritized company-wide — not to run a large
independent testing program. A five-person "growth team" at a 20-person company is usually a sign
of premature specialization, and often duplicates work the core product team should already be
doing.

Host: What's a red flag you look for when hiring a growth leader specifically, someone who'll run
the function?

Tomasz: Someone whose stories are all about channels — "I scaled paid social," "I owned SEO" —
with no story about retention or product influence. The best growth leaders I've worked with think
in terms of the whole funnel and aren't shy about telling the product team "the acquisition
channel isn't the problem, the onboarding is." A growth leader who only ever talks about spend
efficiency on channels will optimize the wrong end of the funnel and blame acquisition quality
when retention doesn't follow.

Host: How do you structure compensation or incentives for a growth team without creating
metric-gaming behavior?

Tomasz: Never comp a growth team purely on a single top-line number like signups or MRR, because
that number can be gamed in ways that damage the business — discounting too aggressively,
acquiring low-intent users, or shipping dark patterns that boost a metric while eroding trust. I'd
rather tie incentives to a small basket of metrics that includes at least one retention or quality
signal alongside the growth number, so gaming one metric visibly hurts another one the same team
is accountable for.

Host: Any final advice for a founder about to make their first growth hire?

Tomasz: Be honest with the candidate, and yourself, about whether you actually have a working
core loop yet. The single most common reason a first growth hire fails and leaves within six
months isn't a bad hire — it's a founder who hired growth to fix a product problem that growth
tactics can't fix. Hiring growth won't retroactively create product-market fit; it will just make
the lack of it easier to measure.
