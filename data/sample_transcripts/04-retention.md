Host: Everyone talks about acquisition, but you've said retention is where most of the
"unexplained" growth actually comes from. Why is it underrated?

Ines: Because it's slow and unglamorous compared to a launch or a campaign. A retention win
doesn't show up as a dramatic spike on a dashboard — it shows up as a curve that's very slightly
less steep than last quarter's curve, compounding over months. Nobody claps for that in a
board meeting, even though the lifetime value impact of flattening a retention curve by five
points often dwarfs anything acquisition can do in the same period, because retention gains
compound across every future cohort, not just the current one.

Host: Where do you start when a team's retention curve is decaying and they don't know why?

Ines: Segment before you diagnose. A single blended retention curve hides at least two very
different stories: users who never really understood the product, and users who understood it
fine but the product stopped being useful for their situation. Those need completely different
fixes — one is an education or onboarding problem, the other might be a real product gap. Cutting
the curve by acquisition channel and by first-week usage depth almost always reveals that
"retention" is really two or three separate problems wearing one number.

Host: What's a retention lever that's cheap to build but consistently gets ignored?

Ines: Re-engagement triggered by user-generated milestones, not company-generated promotions. A
notification that says "you've saved 4 hours this month using X" pulls people back because it's
about their own progress. A notification that says "check out our new feature" pulls people back
because it's about you. The first kind consistently outperforms the second in every product I've
worked on, and it's almost free to build if you're already tracking the underlying usage data.

Host: How do you think about the difference between retention and habit formation?

Ines: Retention is the outcome; habit is the mechanism. A user can be "retained" in the sense of
not churning while still using your product reluctantly, once a month, right before it would
otherwise become useless to them — that's fragile retention, one bad experience away from churn.
Habit means the product is stitched into an existing routine, so usage happens without a
deliberate decision each time. The products with the best long-term retention numbers usually
anchor to something the user was already doing daily — checking a specific app first thing, a
weekly team ritual — rather than trying to invent a new routine from scratch.

Host: What about win-back campaigns for users who've already churned? Do they work?

Ines: They work far better when they're specific about what changed, not generic re-engagement
copy. "We fixed the bug that made X slow" wins back far more churned users than "we miss you,
come back," because a specific reason gives the churned user a concrete reason to reconsider a
decision they already made and moved past. Generic win-back messaging mostly reaches people who
were going to come back anyway, and gets ignored by everyone else.

Host: Any advice on how far ahead a team should measure retention — day 1, day 30, day 90?

Ines: All three, but weight them by what your business actually needs to be true. Day 1
retention tells you if onboarding delivered the promised value. Day 30 tells you if the product
survived contact with the user's real workflow. Day 90 tells you if it's actually sticky enough
to be a durable business. Teams that only watch day 1 optimize for a great first impression that
doesn't hold up; teams that only watch day 90 move too slowly to catch onboarding problems before
they've burned through a quarter of acquisition spend on leaky cohorts.

Host: Last one — what's the biggest retention mistake you see growth teams make?

Ines: Treating retention as a single number to move instead of a curve to reshape. Chasing a
week-4 retention percentage in isolation can lead to shipping something that makes week 4 look
better while making week 12 worse — for example, an artificial engagement loop that keeps people
opening the app without keeping them getting real value. The teams that build durable businesses
watch the whole shape of the curve, not just the one point they report externally.
